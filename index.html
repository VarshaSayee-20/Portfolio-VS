Indian Institute of Science, Bengaluru · GPDK090 90nm · Jan–Oct 2025

Design Challenges & Tradeoffs

  • Final post-route hold slack was razor-thin (+0.050 ns) — required careful hold-aware optimization in Innovus rather than relying on synthesis-stage margin alone.
  • 64 signed Braun multipliers ended up dominating the design, at ~94,498 μm² (46.7%) of the final 202,502.5 μm² cell area — multiplier architecture choice was the single biggest area lever in the whole accelerator.
  • Proving 100% gate-level-vs-golden-model matches across all 319 test samples meant the SDF-back-annotated Xcelium testbench had to exactly mirror the software reference's fixed-point rounding and ReLU behavior — any mismatch there would have looked like a real RTL bug.

What I Took Away

Closing timing at 100 MHz taught me how little hold margin synthesis estimates actually leave for place-and-route to work with, and how much a single architectural choice (the multiplier style) can dominate a PPA budget. Matching gate-level results 1:1 against a software golden model also reinforced how much verification rigor (SDF back-annotation, fixed-point parity) is needed before a number like “100% match” means anything.

Design Challenges & Tradeoffs

  • The comparator's open-loop amplifier had a negative phase margin (−4°), which would ring or oscillate in closed-loop feedback — I had to confirm and justify that this was acceptable only because each comparator is used open-loop as a one-shot slicer, never in closed-loop feedback.

  • Resistor-ladder mismatch was a second, independent source of DNL/INL error on top of comparator offset, so both had to be budgeted against the same ±0.5 LSB monotonicity bound.

What I Took Away

This project sharpened the distinction between a comparator and an amplifier: stability requirements that matter for a closed-loop op-amp (phase margin) don't apply the same way to a one-shot, open-loop slicer. It also taught me to treat offset and mismatch as system-level error budgets (against DNL/INL spec) rather than isolated analog imperfections.

My Contributions

  • Characterized RRAM SET/RESET switching behavior (1T-1R) as the basis for a memristor-based neuromorphic encoding/decoding system.
  • Designed a hybrid RRAM-CMOS Leaky-Integrate-and-Fire neuron, a subthreshold PTAT current reference, an op-amp voltage-to-current converter, a current-mirror array, and an OR-gate spike combiner, all in 180nm CMOS.

Design Challenges & Tradeoffs

  • RRAM SET/RESET switching is inherently variable at the device level, so that variability had to be characterized first and treated as a circuit design constraint, not an afterthought, for the encoder/decoder built on top of it.
  • Keeping total system power at 1.199 mW while still resolving 10 distinct decoder output levels meant the subthreshold PTAT reference and decoding-window generator had to be sized for low quiescent current without losing level-resolution margin.

What I Took Away

Working through device-level RRAM variability up through a full encode/decode system showed me how much neuromorphic temporal coding (the iterative ISI scheme here) depends on tightly budgeting power and noise margin at every stage — a ~45% energy win at the encoder is only meaningful if the decoder can still reliably resolve the resulting signal into distinct levels.

Extreme Learning Machine Algorithm in CMOS

Shiv Nadar University · 180nm CMOS · Spring 2018

My Contributions

  • Implemented a mixed-signal Extreme Learning Machine (ELM) architecture in 180nm CMOS, mapping the algorithm's single-hidden-layer feedforward structure onto an analog/digital current-mode circuit.
  • Designed a current-mode neuron (axon-hillock oscillator) and current-mirror array to realize hidden-layer weights, with a DAC and input-generation circuit to convert digital training inputs into analog drive currents.
  • Built a TSPC (True Single-Phase Clock) D flip-flop based pipeline — flip-flop, counter, carry stage, and PIPO register — to extend the hidden layer via frequency-to-digital rotation counting, avoiding extra analog hardware per hidden node.
  • Verified neuron oscillation frequency vs. drive current, counter/PIPO timing waveforms, and hidden-layer rotation behavior in simulation, and evaluated end-to-end classification accuracy of the resulting ELM network.

This was my first real exercise in mixed-signal partitioning — deciding which parts of an algorithm's structure (here, extending the hidden layer) are cheaper to implement digitally via frequency-to-digital counting than by duplicating analog blocks. It also taught me how tightly digital timing (counter/PIPO) has to be matched to an analog block's behavior (oscillator period) when the two are coupled this directly.

Design Challenges & Tradeoffs

  • With no isolation register between the multiplier and adder, output instability fed into 12-bit register sampling — the fix I proposed was an added pipeline register between the two stages, trading one cycle of latency for higher throughput.
  • Registers in this design aren't resettable, so simulations had to rely on zero-initialization rather than a real hardware reset path — a limitation I had to design around rather than fix within the project's scope.
  • The DPU's effective critical path changed sharply with clocking scheme — ≈1.11 ns with a separately delayed input-register clock vs. ≈2.22 ns (2 cycles) under a single shared clock — so composing the 4×1 and 4×4 arrays meant making a deliberate clocking-architecture choice rather than treating it as a detail.

What I Took Away

Building this array bottom-up (register → multiplier/adder → DPU → 4×1 → 4×4) showed concretely how a missing isolation register or a clocking-scheme choice made at one level of the hierarchy compounds at every level above it. It's the project that first taught me to think about pipeline staging and clock architecture as design decisions, not implementation details to fix later.

5×5 RTL Sparse Matrix Multiply (SpMM) Accelerator

Personal RTL Project · Verilog, CSC/CSR Outer-Product Datapath · ECE 5545 Advanced VLSI Design, Virginia Tech · Spring 2020

A populated ModelSim/QuestaSim waveform dump and simulation library are present alongside this RTL, indicating a simulation was run; I have not independently re-run the simulation myself in this environment, so I'm reporting what the self-checking testbench is designed to verify rather than claiming an observed PASS.

My Contributions

  • Designed and implemented a synthesizable RTL sparse matrix-multiply accelerator in Verilog, parameterized for 5×5 matrices: dense-to-sparse converters, a 5-wide outer-product processing-element array, an 8-input bitonic sort network, a merge/accumulate stage, and a dense accumulator, sequenced by a 4-state finite-state-machine controller.
  • Outer-product dataflow was selected because it naturally exploits sparsity while reducing unnecessary multiply operations compared to dense matrix multiplication.
  • Algorithm: convert the dense input matrices into fixed-slot compressed sparse column and row formats, then for each index from 0 to 4, take the outer product of the corresponding column of A against the row of B, sort the resulting partial products by row and column position via the bitonic network, merge entries that land on the same position, and accumulate the merged updates into a dense 5×5 result matrix.
  • Wrote a self-checking testbench that drives a 7-nonzero sparse A and 10-nonzero sparse B, waits for completion, and compares all 25 output entries against 13 hand-computed golden nonzero values, reporting a pass or fail per mismatch.
  • Independently re-derived the testbench's golden values by hand from the stimulus matrices — all 13 nonzero C entries match, confirming the reference values used to check the design are correct.

Design Challenges & Tradeoffs

  • Mapping a sparse outer-product algorithm onto fixed-width hardware meant committing to fixed-slot compressed sparse column/row storage (a set number of entries per column/row, each tagged valid or not) rather than a fully variable-length sparse format, trading some wasted slots for a regular, indexable datapath.
  • Combining same-position partial products from a 5-wide processing-element array (up to 25 partials per index) without a content-addressable structure required sorting by row and column position first via the bitonic network, so that duplicates become adjacent and can be merged in a single linear pass.

What I Took Away

This project is where I first worked through the tradeoff between general sparse formats and fixed hardware datapaths: a fully variable-length sparse engine would generalize better, but a fixed-slot, statically-scheduled outer-product pipeline (sort-then-merge for collisions) is far simpler to verify exhaustively against hand-computed golden values, which mattered more for a single project's verification budget than asymptotic generality.

My Contributions

  • Designed and assembled two identical analog front-end channels for horizontal and vertical EOG acquisition, using an INA118 instrumentation amplifier with gain 500, a fourth-order Sallen-Key low-pass filter, a second-order high-pass filter, and an op-amp level-shifting stage.
  • Acquired eye-movement signals using five Ag/AgCl electrodes, conditioned them for the microcontroller ADC, and transmitted direction commands to the robot car through an HC-12 wireless module at 115200 bps.
  • Implemented a threshold-based classifier for blink, left, right, and center-fixation states using the horizontal and vertical channel measurements.
  • Characterized the circuit in LTspice and on hardware, recording repeatable horizontal, vertical, and blink waveforms before and after digital conversion.

Design Challenges & Tradeoffs

  • EOG is sensitive to baseline drift, power-line interference, electrode placement, gaze angle, and eye strain; the analog band-pass response and 0–5 V level shift were necessary to produce a stable signal for the microcontroller ADC.
  • The threshold classifier was simple enough for the embedded prototype, but its thresholds required manual adjustment as electrode sensitivity changed.
  • The prototype demonstrated acquisition, classification, and direction control, while wireless transmission delay still needed refinement for faster command updates.

What I Took Away

This project connected analog circuit design directly to a physical control system: electrode placement, low-frequency filtering, level shifting, embedded classification, and wireless timing all had to work together before an eye movement could become a reliable robot command.
